Language and Its Need

No human society, however small or isolated, has ever been found without language. This raises an important question: why does every human society need language at all?

The Learners Ladder Editorial Team | 9 min read | Linguistics

Every known culture — from remote rainforest communities to vast modern cities — has developed a complex system of communication. This guide examines the six deep needs language fulfils, and why nothing else can fulfil them in its place.

1.The Need to Share Thought

The most basic need language fulfils is making the private public. A thought, by its nature, exists only inside one mind. Without language, that thought would remain permanently locked away, unknowable to anyone else.

Language solves this by converting internal mental states into external signals — sounds or symbols — that another mind can receive and reconstruct into a similar thought. Two people who have never met, who grew up in entirely different environments, can come to hold remarkably similar ideas in their minds simply through an exchange of words.

2.The Need for Cooperation and Survival

Early human communities depended heavily on coordinated group activity — hunting large animals, building shelters, defending against danger, and sharing food. None of these activities can be carried out efficiently without communication.

With language, a group can plan in advance, assign roles, warn of danger from a distance, and adapt their plan as circumstances change. This need for cooperation has not disappeared with modern civilisation — it has simply changed form in workplaces, governments, hospitals and schools.

3.The Need to Transmit Knowledge Across Generations

Every generation of humans does not have to rediscover fire, reinvent agriculture, or relearn medicine from nothing. Knowledge accumulated by one generation can be passed, through language, to the next — and the next after that.

Cultural Transmission

This process is what separates human progress from that of other species. A human child can be told directly, in words, exactly how and why something works — and can ask questions to deepen understanding further. Without language, every generation would begin from zero.

4.The Need for Abstract Thought

Language does more than describe the physical world — it allows humans to construct and manipulate ideas that have no physical form at all. Concepts such as justice, democracy, mathematics, time, and morality exist because language gives them a name and a shape that can be discussed, debated and refined.

Complex legal systems, scientific theories, and philosophical arguments could not exist without a language sophisticated enough to express conditional reasoning, hypothetical situations, and abstract relationships between ideas.

5.The Need for Identity and Belonging

The language a person speaks is closely tied to their sense of identity, community and belonging. Shared language allows individuals to recognise others as part of their group, fostering trust and cooperation.

This is why the loss of a language is often experienced as a profound cultural loss. When a language disappears, an entire way of understanding and describing the world disappears with it — including unique words, idioms, and ways of expressing relationships that may have no equivalent in any other language.

6.The Need for Emotional Expression

Language gives humans the ability to express internal emotional states with precision far beyond a simple cry, laugh or gesture. A person can explain not just that they feel sad, but precisely why — describing loss, disappointment, longing or grief in nuanced and specific terms.

This precision allows for deeper emotional understanding between people, strengthening relationships and enabling empathy in ways that would be far more limited without language.

A Thought Experiment: Could Humans Function Without Language?

Without language, there would be no spoken instruction, no written record, no shared history, no complex law, no science as we understand it, and severely limited cooperation. Every individual would be confined largely to their own direct experience, unable to benefit from the accumulated knowledge of others.

This illustrates why language is not simply a convenient tool that humans happen to use, but a fundamental requirement for human society to exist in anything resembling its current form.

Real-World Example

Consider a hospital emergency room. A doctor shares thought by describing symptoms to a colleague; the team cooperates by coordinating roles under language-based instructions; medical knowledge, built up over centuries, is transmitted through training and textbooks; the diagnosis itself relies on abstract concepts like "infection" or "risk"; the hospital staff share a professional identity built partly on shared medical language; and a doctor's calm, precise words to a frightened patient rely on emotional expression. All six needs, working together, in a single ordinary event.

Why This Matters

The needs that language fulfils are not separate or independent — they build upon each other. Sharing thought enables cooperation, cooperation enables knowledge transmission, and transmitted knowledge enables the abstract ideas that make human civilisation possible. Language is not something humans invented to make life easier. It is something without which human life, as we know it, would not exist at all.
