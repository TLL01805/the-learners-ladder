What is Health? Physical, Mental & Social Wellbeing

Most people think "being healthy" just means not being sick. But real health is much bigger than that — it covers your body, your mind, and your relationships with others.

The Learners Ladder Editorial Team | 9 min read | Health

In 1948, the World Health Organization (WHO) gave the world a definition of health that is still used today, and it changed how doctors, teachers, and governments think about wellbeing. This article explains what health really means, why it has three connected parts, and how understanding this can help you look after yourself better.

1.Defining Health

For a long time, people believed health simply meant the absence of disease — if you weren't ill, you were healthy. The WHO challenged this idea with a much broader definition:

Official Definition

"Health is a state of complete physical, mental, and social wellbeing, and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity." — World Health Organization, 1948

This means a person could have no illness at all, yet still not be fully healthy — for example, if they are constantly stressed, lonely, or unable to maintain healthy relationships.

2.The Three Dimensions of Health

Health is usually broken down into three connected dimensions. Each one affects the others, so looking after all three is essential for genuine wellbeing.

The three overlapping dimensions of health — physical, mental, and social wellbeing.

3.Physical Health

Physical health refers to the condition of your body — how well your organs, muscles, and systems function, and how effectively your body can fight off illness and recover from injury.

  • Nutrition — eating a balanced variety of foods to fuel the body
  • Exercise — regular movement to strengthen the heart, muscles, and bones
  • Sleep — allowing the body time to repair and recharge
  • Hygiene — preventing the spread of infection and disease
  • Avoiding harmful substances — such as tobacco, alcohol, and drugs

4.Mental Health

Mental health is about how you think, feel, and cope with everyday life. It includes your emotional wellbeing, your ability to manage stress, and your capacity to make decisions and solve problems.

Example

A student who feels calm and confident before an exam, and who can manage nervousness without it becoming overwhelming, is displaying good mental health — even if they don't get a perfect score.

Good mental health does not mean being happy all the time. It means having the resilience and tools to handle sadness, stress, or setbacks in a healthy way.

5.Social Health

Social health refers to your ability to form satisfying relationships with others and to interact positively within your community, family, and friendship groups.

DimensionFocusExample Habit
PhysicalThe body's condition and functionEating a balanced diet
MentalThoughts, emotions, and coping abilityTalking about problems instead of bottling them up
SocialRelationships and community connectionSpending quality time with family and friends
Real-World Example

Consider two students who both catch a cold. Student A eats well, sleeps enough, talks to friends about feeling under the weather, and recovers within a week feeling supported. Student B skips meals, stays up late worrying alone, and avoids friends out of embarrassment. Both had the same illness, but Student A's stronger physical, mental, and social habits led to a faster, easier recovery — showing how the three dimensions work together in real life.

A Closing Thought

Health is not a single switch that is either "on" or "off." It is a balance of body, mind, and relationships that needs continuous care. Understanding all three dimensions helps you recognise that looking after your mental and social wellbeing is just as important as looking after your physical body.
