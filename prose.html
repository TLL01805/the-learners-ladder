Prose: The Complete Guide

Almost everything you read — novels, news articles, emails, this very sentence — is prose. Here is what actually defines it, its major forms, and the elements that make it work.

The Learners Ladder Editorial Team | 17 min read | Literature

Prose is language in its most natural shape: ordinary grammar, ordinary sentences, flowing the way we actually speak and think. It is so common that most readers never stop to ask what it actually is — until they compare it with poetry.

This guide defines prose precisely, walks through its major categories — fiction and nonfiction — and their subtypes, explains the core elements every strong piece of prose relies on, and covers the different styles a writer can choose depending on purpose.

1.What Is Prose?

Prose is written or spoken language in its ordinary form, structured in sentences and paragraphs, following the natural grammatical flow of the language — without the deliberate rhythm, meter, or line breaks that define poetry.

Example

"The rain had not stopped for three days. Sarah watched it from the kitchen window, wondering whether the roads would still be passable by morning." — ordinary sentences, ordinary grammar, arranged into paragraphs. This is prose.

2.Prose vs. Poetry: The Core Difference

The clearest way to understand prose is by contrast. Poetry deliberately shapes language through line breaks, rhythm, and often rhyme; prose does not rely on any of these and instead flows continuously, the way natural speech does.

FeatureProsePoetry
StructureSentences and paragraphsLines and stanzas
RhythmNatural, unforcedOften deliberate (meter)
Line breaksDetermined by margin/lengthChosen by the poet for effect
RhymeRare, usually unintentionalOften present, though not required

Our companion guide on poetry covers the other side of this comparison in full detail.

3.The Two Major Categories: Fiction and Nonfiction

All prose falls into one of two broad categories, based on a single question: is it imagined, or is it real?

Fiction

Prose that comes from the imagination — invented characters, events, and settings, even when inspired by real life. Novels, short stories, and novellas are all fiction.

Nonfiction

Prose based on real people, events, and facts. Essays, biographies, journalism, and speeches are all nonfiction.

4.Types of Fiction Prose

  • Novel: a long, fully developed fictional narrative, typically 60,000+ words, with a complex plot and multiple characters.
  • Novella: shorter than a novel but longer than a short story, usually 20,000–50,000 words, often focused on a single, tightly developed idea.
  • Short story: a compact, complete fictional narrative — usually under 10,000 words — built around a single conflict or moment.
  • Flash fiction: an extremely short story, sometimes under 1,000 words, that captures a complete narrative arc in a tiny space.

5.Types of Nonfiction Prose

  • Essay: a focused piece of writing exploring a single idea, argument, or reflection.
  • Biography / Autobiography: the true account of a person's life, written by someone else (biography) or by themselves (autobiography).
  • Journalism: factual reporting of current events, structured for clarity and objectivity.
  • Speech: prose composed to be delivered aloud, often persuasive or ceremonial in purpose.
  • Memoir: a personal account focused on specific memories or a particular period, rather than an entire life.

6.The Core Elements of Prose Fiction

Strong fictional prose is built from five interconnected elements, each covered in depth in our Creative Writing series.

ElementWhat It Does
PlotThe sequence of events, usually built on the three-act structure
CharacterThe people who drive and experience the events
SettingThe time and place the story unfolds in
Point of viewWho is telling the story, and from what vantage point
ThemeThe underlying idea or message the story explores

7.Point of View in Prose

Point of view determines how much the reader is allowed to know, and through whose eyes.

First Person

The narrator is a character in the story, using "I." "I opened the door slowly, unsure of what I would find."

Third Person Limited

The narrator follows one character closely, using "he/she/they," but only knows that character's thoughts. "She opened the door slowly, unsure of what she would find."

Third Person Omniscient

The narrator knows the thoughts and feelings of every character. "She opened the door slowly. Behind it, her brother held his breath, terrified she would notice him."

8.Styles and Purposes of Prose

Beyond fiction and nonfiction, prose can also be classified by its underlying purpose or technique:

  • Narrative prose: tells a story, with events unfolding over time (most fiction, memoirs).
  • Descriptive prose: focuses on painting a vivid picture using sensory detail, often slowing the pace to build atmosphere.
  • Expository prose: explains or informs, aiming for clarity above style (textbooks, news reports, how-to guides).
  • Persuasive prose: aims to convince the reader of a viewpoint, using argument and evidence (opinion essays, speeches).
Same Idea, Four Styles

Narrative: "I walked into the storm without a coat, and by the time I reached home, I was soaked through." Descriptive: "Rain hammered the pavement in cold, relentless sheets, soaking through fabric within seconds." Expository: "Heavy rainfall can rapidly saturate clothing, leading to a significant drop in body temperature." Persuasive: "No one should have to walk home in weather this severe — better shelter provisions are clearly needed."

9.Rhythm in Prose

Prose does not use meter the way poetry does, but skilled prose writers still shape rhythm deliberately — through sentence length, punctuation, and word choice — to control pacing and emotional effect.

Example: Controlling Pace

Short, urgent sentences: "She ran. The door slammed. Silence." Long, flowing sentences: "She wandered slowly through the quiet garden, unhurried, letting the scent of late summer settle over her like a memory she couldn't quite place."

Real-World Example

Consider the opening of a short story: "The house had been empty for eleven years, but Maya still remembered which floorboard creaked, which door stuck in humid weather, which window let in the morning light exactly the way she liked it. Some places you never really leave, even after you've gone." This is narrative, third-person-limited prose — following one character's perspective, using ordinary sentence structure, with rhythm shaped by a short final sentence for emotional weight. Every element covered in this guide is visible in miniature: plot implied through backstory, character revealed through detail, setting anchoring the mood, and theme quietly stated in the final line.

Why This Matters

Recognising prose — and knowing its categories, elements, and styles — is the foundation for reading almost everything you will ever encounter, and for writing almost everything you will ever produce, from a novel to a work email. Understanding how prose differs from poetry, covered next in this series, sharpens your sense of both.
