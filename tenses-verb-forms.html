Tenses & Verb Forms: The Complete Guide

Every verb tells the reader not just what happened, but when. Here are all twelve English tenses, irregular verbs, voice, mood, and modals — explained clearly, with examples.

The Learners Ladder Editorial Team | 16 min read | Grammar

Tense is how English marks time. Get it right, and a reader knows exactly when something happened, is happening, or will happen — without a single extra word.

English has three basic time frames — past, present, and future — and four aspects within each: simple, continuous, perfect, and perfect continuous. Multiply these together and you get the twelve tenses that this guide covers in full, along with irregular verbs, active and passive voice, mood, and modal verbs.

1.The Three Time Frames

Before the twelve tenses, it helps to see the three broad categories they all fall into:

  • Past: something that already happened
  • Present: something happening now, generally true, or habitual
  • Future: something that has not yet happened

2.The Four Aspects

Each time frame can be expressed in four different ways, depending on how the action relates to time:

  • Simple: a plain fact or single action ("She walks.")
  • Continuous (Progressive): an action in progress ("She is walking.")
  • Perfect: an action completed before another point in time ("She has walked.")
  • Perfect Continuous: an action that continued up to a point in time ("She has been walking.")

3.Present Tenses

Present Simple

Use: habits, facts, schedules. Form: base verb (+s for third person). Example: "She walks to school every day." / "Water boils at 100°C."

Present Continuous

Use: actions happening right now, or fixed future plans. Form: am/is/are + verb-ing. Example: "She is walking to school right now." / "We are leaving tomorrow."

Present Perfect

Use: past action with present relevance, or unfinished time up to now. Form: have/has + past participle. Example: "She has walked ten miles today."

Present Perfect Continuous

Use: an action that started in the past and continues now, emphasising duration. Form: have/has been + verb-ing. Example: "She has been walking for two hours."

4.Past Tenses

Past Simple

Use: a completed action at a specific past time. Form: verb + ed (or irregular form). Example: "She walked to school yesterday."

Past Continuous

Use: an action in progress at a specific past moment, often interrupted by another. Form: was/were + verb-ing. Example: "She was walking when it started to rain."

Past Perfect

Use: an action completed before another past action. Form: had + past participle. Example: "She had walked five miles before she stopped to rest."

Past Perfect Continuous

Use: an ongoing action that continued up to a point in the past. Form: had been + verb-ing. Example: "She had been walking for an hour before she felt tired."

5.Future Tenses

Future Simple

Use: a decision made now, a prediction, or a promise. Form: will + base verb. Example: "She will walk to school tomorrow."

Future Continuous

Use: an action that will be in progress at a specific future time. Form: will be + verb-ing. Example: "She will be walking to school at 8 a.m."

Future Perfect

Use: an action that will be completed before a specific future time. Form: will have + past participle. Example: "By 9 a.m., she will have walked two miles."

Future Perfect Continuous

Use: an action that will continue up to a specific point in the future. Form: will have been + verb-ing. Example: "By 9 a.m., she will have been walking for two hours."

7.Active and Passive Voice

Voice shows whether the subject performs the action (active) or receives it (passive).

Examples

Active: "The chef cooked the meal." (subject performs the action) Passive: "The meal was cooked by the chef." (subject receives the action)

Passive voice is formed with a form of "to be" + past participle, and is useful when the action matters more than who performed it — for example, in scientific or formal writing: "The samples were tested under controlled conditions."

8.Mood: Indicative, Imperative, Subjunctive

Mood shows the speaker's attitude toward what is being said.

  • Indicative: states a fact or opinion. "She works hard."
  • Imperative: gives a command or request. "Work harder."
  • Subjunctive: expresses a wish, suggestion, or hypothetical. "I wish she were here." / "It is essential that he arrive on time."

9.Modal Verbs

Modals combine with a base verb to express ability, permission, possibility, obligation, or advice. They never change form (no -s, -ing, or -ed) and are always followed by the base verb.

10.Common Tense Errors

  • Tense shift: switching tense without reason mid-sentence or mid-paragraph. Incorrect: "She walks to school and then ate lunch." Correct: "She walked to school and then ate lunch."
  • Confusing past simple and present perfect: Incorrect: "I have seen him yesterday." Correct: "I saw him yesterday," since a specific past time word ("yesterday") requires past simple, not present perfect.
  • Irregular verb errors: Incorrect: "She goed to the store." Correct: "She went to the store."
Real-World Example

Consider this line from a project update email: "By the time you read this, the team will have finished testing, and the results will be shared by Friday." The first clause uses the future perfect ("will have finished") to show an action completed before a future point, while the second uses the future simple passive ("will be shared") because the focus is on the results, not on who is sharing them. Choosing the right tense and voice here makes the timeline of the project instantly clear to the reader.

Why This Matters

Tense is what places every sentence you write in time. Once you know the parts of speech (Topic I), how to structure a sentence (Topic II), and how to punctuate it (Topic III), tense and verb form (Topic IV) is what lets you say precisely when something happened — a small grammatical choice that has a large effect on how clearly a reader understands you.
