Formal vs. Informal English: The Complete Guide

The same idea can be said a dozen different ways — and choosing the right register is often what separates a message that lands well from one that doesn't.

The Learners Ladder Editorial Team | 14 min read | Vocabulary

"Can you help me out?" and "I would be grateful for your assistance" mean exactly the same thing — but you would never use the second one texting a friend, or the first one in a job application.

This distinction is called register: the level of formality a speaker or writer chooses based on the situation, audience, and purpose. This guide covers how formal and informal English differ across vocabulary, grammar, contractions, and tone, with side-by-side examples throughout.

1.What Is Register?

Register is the degree of formality in language, adjusted according to the situation. English speakers constantly shift register without thinking about it — the same person might text a friend informally, then write a formal email an hour later.

Example

Informal (text to a friend): "Hey, can't make it tonight, sorry!" Formal (email to a colleague): "I am writing to let you know that I am unable to attend this evening. My apologies for the inconvenience."

2.When to Use Formal English

  • Academic essays and research papers
  • Business and professional emails
  • Official letters and reports
  • Job applications and cover letters
  • Public speeches and presentations
  • Legal and government documents

3.When to Use Informal English

  • Conversations with friends and family
  • Text messages and casual social media posts
  • Personal blogs or diaries
  • Everyday spoken conversation

4.Contractions

One of the clearest markers of register: informal English uses contractions freely; formal English generally avoids them.

Examples

Informal: "I don't think it's going to work, and we can't fix it in time." Formal: "I do not believe it will work, and we cannot fix it in time."

5.Vocabulary Choices

Formal English favours longer, often Latin-derived words, while informal English favours shorter, everyday words and phrasal verbs.

6.Phrasal Verbs vs. Single-Word Verbs

Informal English relies heavily on phrasal verbs; formal English tends to replace them with a single, more precise verb.

Examples

Informal: "We need to look into this problem and figure out a solution." Formal: "We need to investigate this problem and determine a solution."

7.Passive Voice

Formal writing — especially academic and scientific writing — uses the passive voice more often, to keep focus on the action or result rather than the person performing it. Informal English strongly prefers the active voice.

Examples

Informal (active): "We tested the sample three times." Formal (passive): "The sample was tested three times."

8.Modal Verbs and Politeness

Formal English uses more tentative, indirect modal phrasing to sound polite and professional; informal English is often more direct.

Examples

Informal: "Can you send me the file?" Formal: "Would you be able to send me the file?" or "I would appreciate it if you could send me the file."

9.Slang, Idioms, and Filler Words

Slang ("that's lit"), casual idioms ("piece of cake"), and filler words ("like," "kind of," "you know") belong almost exclusively to informal English. Formal writing avoids them entirely in favour of precise, literal language.

Example

Informal: "Honestly, this project was kind of a piece of cake once we got going." Formal: "This project proved to be relatively straightforward once underway."

10.Sentence Length and Structure

Informal English often uses short, fragmented, or run-together sentences that mimic natural speech. Formal English favours complete, well-structured sentences — frequently complex or compound-complex, as covered in our sentence structure guide.

12.Common Mistakes

  • Mixing registers within the same piece: starting a formal email with "Hi" and ending it with "Cheers" alongside otherwise formal language creates an inconsistent tone.
  • Being overly formal in casual contexts: texting a friend "I would be grateful if you could confirm your arrival time" sounds stiff and unnatural.
  • Using slang in professional writing: phrases like "that's a bummer" or "no biggie" undermine credibility in a business report.
Real-World Example

Compare a text to a friend and a formal complaint email describing the same problem. Text: "Ugh, my order still hasn't shown up, so annoying!" Formal email: "I am writing to inform you that my order has not yet arrived, and I would appreciate an update on its status." Both express frustration about the same delay, but only the second is appropriate for a business context — no contractions, no slang, and precise, professional vocabulary throughout.

Why This Matters

Knowing grammar and vocabulary is not enough on its own — knowing which register fits which situation is what makes communication actually effective. A perfectly grammatical sentence can still feel wrong if it is too casual for a job application or too stiff for a text to a friend. Mastering register is what turns correct English into genuinely appropriate English.
